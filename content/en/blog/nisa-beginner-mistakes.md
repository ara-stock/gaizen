## One Warning for Beginners: Do Not Start With Individual Stocks

Soon after starting investing, many people want to buy an individual stock.

A stock repeatedly discussed on social media, a company offering attractive shareholder benefits, or a high displayed dividend yield can feel more exciting than an index fund. Choosing it yourself feels active, and the potential profit appears larger.

If I could give a beginner only one warning, it would be: **do not make an individual stock your first investment**.

The reason is not that individual stocks always lose money. It is that beginners usually do not yet have the **comparison framework** needed to judge whether a decision is good or bad.

> If you have not yet created a brokerage account and recurring contribution, start with "[Your First 30 Days of Investing in Japan](/en/blog/investing-first-30-days)." This article is the mistake-prevention guide for after automation begins.

## Individual Stock Decisions Require Comparisons

Before buying an individual stock, compare at least the following:

- How widely the stock normally moves
- How its historical gains and declines compare with peers
- Whether its market capitalization matches the scale and volatility of the business
- Which business produces profit and whether it can continue doing so
- How revenue, margins, and growth compare with competitors
- What you will do if the share price falls

If you have reviewed only one company, you cannot tell whether its valuation is high, its margins are weak, or its volatility is unusual.

The main problem is not simply lacking knowledge. It is **committing meaningful capital before having standards for evaluating the decision**.

:::comment
My first individual stock purchase was based on shareholder benefits and dividend yield, without understanding the company. I had no comparison framework, so I could not judge whether the stock was actually attractive.

Buying individual stocks is not inherently wrong. But if you cannot compare volatility, business scale, operations, and profitability with other companies, you will not know what to do when the price falls.
:::

## Mistake 1: Buying Only Because a Stock Is Famous Online

Repeatedly seeing a stock on social media can create the feeling that you have researched it. Knowing the name, however, is not the same as understanding the business.

Social posts may omit:

- The author's purchase price and holding period
- The position's weight within the full portfolio
- The amount of loss the author can tolerate
- The author's exit conditions
- Advertising, referral compensation, or existing positions

A popular stock can be a starting point for research. Popularity itself should not be the purchase reason.

Before buying, confirm that you can explain how the company earns money, what advantage it has over competitors, and under what conditions you would sell.

## Mistake 2: Selecting Only for Dividend Yield or Shareholder Benefits

High dividend yield and shareholder benefits can be attractive, but neither establishes that a company is suitable for long-term ownership.

A dividend yield can rise because the share price fell, even when the dividend did not increase. If earnings deteriorate, the company may reduce or eliminate the dividend. Shareholder benefit programs can also be changed or discontinued.

Look at the business behind the payout:

- Does the core business produce sustainable profit?
- Is the payout ratio manageable?
- Is the company funding dividends through borrowing or asset sales?
- Would you still own the company without the shareholder benefit?

See "[How to Build a High-Dividend Portfolio](/en/blog/high-dividend-portfolio)" for a fuller checklist.

## Mistake 3: Putting a Large Amount Into a Product You Do Not Understand

Choose investments based not only on potential returns, but also on how much they can fall.

I once owned a leveraged NASDAQ100 fund and sold at a loss because I could not tolerate its volatility. Holding it longer would later have produced a large gain, but that is hindsight.

The real problem was not selling. It was choosing a product and position size that I could not continue holding while losses grew.

Leveraged products generally aim to deliver a multiple of an index's **daily** movement. Returns over multiple days do not necessarily equal that same multiple, and repeated market swings can reduce fund value.

Before buying, answer:

- Could I continue holding after a 30% decline?
- Would a 50% decline affect my life or sleep?
- Can I explain why I own this product?
- What conditions would cause me to sell?

Do not put a large amount into a product if you cannot answer those questions.

## Mistake 4: Trading Too Frequently

I have repeatedly sold after a modest gain, only to realize that holding longer would have produced much more profit.

Taking profit is not automatically a mistake. But buying without a planned holding period or exit condition, then reacting to every small rise and decline, can cause investors to miss long-term business growth.

NISA exempts eligible gains from Japanese tax, but selling does not restore the current year's annual investment allowance. The acquisition value of the sold position restores lifetime allowance from the following year, still subject to annual limits.

If you use NISA for individual stocks, long-term holdings generally fit the system better than short-term trading.

Before selling, identify which condition applies:

1. The original investment thesis failed
2. Business or financial conditions deteriorated
3. The position became too large within the portfolio
4. You need cash for an upcoming expense
5. You simply became anxious after looking at the price

If only the fifth condition applies, pause and review the original purchase reason before trading.

## Mistake 5: Stopping Contributions Because Markets Fell

I have continued my index-fund contributions through market declines rather than stopping or selling.

Recurring investing automatically purchases a predetermined amount. Stopping only after prices fall also stops purchases at lower prices.

However, household finances come first. If living expenses or emergency savings become insufficient, adjust contributions regardless of market direction.

The goal is not to "always invest through every decline." It is to **base the contribution amount on household conditions rather than market emotion**.

## Looking at Prices Is Not the Problem

When I started investing, I checked prices almost every day. I still look at markets relatively frequently.

The purpose has changed.

Today, I mainly check whether companies on my watchlist have fallen to prices where I want to research or buy them. I pay little attention to daily changes in my existing holdings.

Looking at a price is not the problem. Feeling compelled to trade each time you look is the problem.

### A Practical Review Schedule for Beginners

| Item | What to Review | Suggested Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Index-fund contribution | Correct account, fund, and amount | Monthly |
| Individual stock | Earnings and material company disclosures | At announcements |
| Watchlist | Whether a predefined purchase condition is approaching | Based on your rules |
| Daily unrealized gain/loss | Do not use alone as a trading reason | Only when necessary |

## Checklist Before Buying in NISA

- [ ] The stock was not selected only because it is famous online
- [ ] There is a reason beyond dividend yield or shareholder benefits
- [ ] You can explain the business and source of profit
- [ ] You compared market capitalization, margins, and growth with peers
- [ ] The position size would not affect your life after a major decline
- [ ] You defined exit conditions before purchasing
- [ ] There is a reason to hold for the long term
- [ ] Your index-fund contribution is already sustainable

If you cannot answer one of these, continue the index contribution and research before buying.

## Summary

- Beginners lack comparison standards, making individual-stock quality difficult to judge
- Do not buy only because a stock is famous online, offers benefits, or displays a high yield
- Avoid products and position sizes whose declines you cannot tolerate
- Frequent trading can cause you to miss long-term growth
- NISA generally fits long-term holdings better than short-term trading
- Base contributions on household finances rather than market direction
- Separate the reason for checking prices from the conditions for trading

:::comment
I have sold too early through frequent trading and missed later gains. I also sold a leveraged NASDAQ fund because I could not tolerate the growing loss.

That does not mean investors should simply endure every decline. The important lesson is to avoid choosing a product or position size that you cannot realistically continue holding.

Beginners should first experience price movement and recurring contributions through an index fund. During that time, compare multiple companies and build standards. There is plenty of time to buy individual stocks afterward.
:::
