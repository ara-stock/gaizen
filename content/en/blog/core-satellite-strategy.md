## What Is the Core-Satellite Strategy?

> **July 2026 update:** This article records how I expanded from investment trusts into individual stocks. I no longer use the core-satellite split as the foundation of my decisions. Today I evaluate long-term holdings primarily through business growth and durability. The ratios below describe the portfolio when this article was written.

The core-satellite approach splits a portfolio into two distinct layers:

- **Core**: The stable foundation. Primarily index funds and investment trusts targeting market-average returns.
- **Satellite**: The high-upside layer. Individual stocks and thematic bets aimed at outperforming the market.

General explanations often use a 70–80% core and 20–30% satellite split as an example. When this article was written, my portfolio sat at roughly 40% core and individual stocks carried more weight. That ratio was not planned from the start; it evolved over time.

---

## Why I Started With Index Funds

When I began investing, two obstacles stood in the way of buying individual stocks.

**1. Limited knowledge of US individual stocks**

Picking stocks, reading earnings reports, understanding sector dynamics — I didn't have the tools or the confidence to analyze individual US companies properly.

**2. I wanted to avoid complex tax procedures**

US dividends are subject to a 10% withholding tax. To reclaim that, you need to file a foreign tax credit claim — a paperwork overhead I preferred to skip at the start.

So I built the core first, using index funds. An index gives you the market average — no catastrophic misses. And once the amounts get large, the idea of having everything in a single stock becomes uncomfortable. An unexpected bankruptcy or fraud case could wipe out the position entirely.

> **The core is where I put money I want to feel safe about.** Not because I expect it to outperform, but because I don't need it to — I just need it not to blow up.

## My First Satellite Move: Japan Tobacco (JT)

Once I had enough investing experience to feel grounded, I started adding individual positions.

One of my early calls was **Japan Tobacco (JT / 2914)**.

At the time, just before the new NISA system launched in Japan, a flood of NISA-related articles was pulling in first-time investors. I reasoned that as retail participation grew, high-dividend stocks and shareholder benefit stocks would attract attention. JT fit that thesis well.

The trade played out. JT consistently ranked among the top individual stock purchases in NISA growth investment accounts. Its dividend rose to ¥234 per share for FY2025 (up ¥40 year-over-year), pushing the yield to approximately 4.75%.

Around the same time, I also looked at shareholder benefit stocks and, counterintuitively, **stocks that announced the discontinuation of their benefit programs**. When benefits are cancelled, the stock often sells off — but if the company redirects the cost to increased dividends, it can become an attractive entry point for long-term holders.

Post-COVID recovery demand and a broader global re-rating of Japanese equities also provided tailwinds through this period.

## How I Choose Satellite Positions

My satellite picks tend to share two characteristics:

**① Not part of the S&P 500**

If I'm already accumulating an S&P 500 index fund as my core, there's limited incremental value in adding individual S&P 500 names to the satellite layer. The interesting satellite territory is what the index doesn't cover — Japanese equities, specific themes, niche sectors.

**② A clear 1–2 year thesis**

Satellites aren't long-term set-and-forget positions. They're tied to a specific hypothesis: a policy shift, a consumer trend, an expanding investor base. I need a concrete reason why something should move within a defined timeframe.

---

## Core and Satellite Can Overlap

One thing I've come to believe: **there's nothing wrong with holding the same name in both layers**.

Google (Alphabet), Apple, Amazon, and Microsoft are in my core through S&P 500 index exposure. I also hold them individually at higher weights in my satellite layer. The logic: if I have high conviction in a specific name, adding to it beyond the index weighting is a deliberate, active call — not a redundancy.

"It's in the index, so I can't put it in my satellite" is a false constraint. The question is whether you have a reason to tilt beyond market weight.

---

## The Ratio Matters Less Than the Habit

General guidance says keep the core at 70–80%. That's a starting point, not a law.

What matters more is gradually developing the ability to **manage drawdowns and concentration risk** in a way that feels natural. Jumping straight into individual stocks with a large portfolio is how investors get shaken out early. Starting with a strong core, then expanding into satellites as your judgment matures — that sequence tends to produce investors who stick around.

:::comment
Starting with index funds was the right call. If I had gone straight to individual stocks without the foundation, I'd have been trading on instinct with no framework — probably out of the market within a year.

With JT, I wasn't buying because I loved the company. I was reading the flow: new investors arrive, high-dividend stocks get crowded, JT fits the profile. Realizing that "which situation is setting up" matters more to me than "which company do I believe in" was a useful self-discovery.

Holding GAFAM in both core and satellite might look redundant from the outside. But to me it's simple: I get the market rate through the index, and then I deliberately add more where I have the most conviction.
:::
