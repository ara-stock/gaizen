## The Big Three: VYM, HDV, SPYD

These three ETFs dominate the conversation whenever US high-dividend investing comes up. Each one has a different philosophy, risk profile, and use case.

| ETF | Issuer | Expense Ratio | Holdings | Approx. Yield |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| VYM | Vanguard | 0.06% | ~550 stocks | 2.8–3.2% |
| HDV | BlackRock (iShares) | 0.08% | ~75 stocks | 3.2–3.8% |
| SPYD | State Street | 0.07% | ~80 stocks | 4.0–4.8% |

*Yields fluctuate with market conditions.*

## VYM: The Default Choice for Stability

VYM (Vanguard High Dividend Yield ETF) tracks the FTSE High Dividend Yield Index, which includes US stocks with above-average projected dividend yields.

### What makes it stand out

**Broad diversification across ~550 stocks.** No single company dominates the portfolio, which limits the damage when individual names struggle.

**0.06% expense ratio — among the lowest in its category.** Cost differences compound over time. VYM's edge here is consistent.

**Lower volatility than SPYD.** During the March 2020 crash, VYM fell significantly less than SPYD. The broad diversification acts as a natural buffer.

### Best for

- First-time high-dividend ETF buyers
- Investors who want dividend income without sacrificing growth exposure
- Long-term holders who want minimal complexity

## HDV: Quality Screen in the Middle

HDV (iShares Core High Dividend ETF) from BlackRock takes a more selective approach.

### What makes it stand out

**Morningstar's economic moat screening.** Rather than simply picking the highest-yielding stocks, HDV filters for financial health and dividend sustainability. With ~75 holdings, it's concentrated but intentionally so.

**Heavy weight toward defensive sectors.** Energy, healthcare, and consumer staples dominate. This gives HDV relatively strong performance during economic downturns, but it lags in strong growth markets.

**Yield sits between VYM and SPYD.** It's the middle ground on both risk and return.

### Best for

- Investors who prioritize dividend sustainability over maximizing current yield
- Those building recession-resilient exposure
- Anyone wanting more energy and healthcare tilt

## SPYD: Maximum Yield, Maximum Volatility

SPYD (SPDR Portfolio S&P 500 High Dividend ETF) from State Street takes the most aggressive yield approach.

### What makes it stand out

**Equal-weight allocation across the top 80 highest-yielding S&P 500 stocks.** Because it selects by yield rather than market cap, SPYD ends up concentrated in financials, real estate, and energy — classic value sectors.

**Highest yield of the three.** If dividend income is the primary objective, SPYD delivers the most.

**Highest volatility of the three.** The equal-weight, high-yield structure means sharp drawdowns in downturns. In March 2020, SPYD fell over 10 percentage points more than VYM. Holdings rotate regularly, which adds additional instability compared to the other two.

### Best for

- Investors actively maximizing quarterly dividend income
- Those comfortable with higher short-term volatility
- Investors who want deliberate exposure to value and cyclical sectors

## How to Choose: A Simple Framework

The tradeoff is straightforward — yield versus stability.

```
Stability-focused → VYM (diversified, cheap, smooth ride)
Balance-focused   → HDV (quality screen, moderate yield)
Yield-focused     → SPYD (maximum income, higher swings)
```

If you're new to high-dividend ETFs, VYM is the clearest starting point. Low cost, broad diversification, and reasonable yield check the fundamentals for long-term ownership.

Adding SPYD for additional yield or HDV for recession exposure are reasonable second steps — not where you start.

## A Note for NISA Account Holders (Japan)

All three are US-listed ETFs, so they go into the Growth Investment Quota of NISA (not the recurring investment quota).

Two things to know:

1. **US withholding tax (10%) is not recoverable inside NISA.** Japan's NISA eliminates Japanese domestic taxes, but the 10% withheld at source by the US cannot be offset through foreign tax credits when held in a NISA account. You effectively pay that 10% regardless.

2. **Currency conversion costs apply** — either explicitly when you convert yen to dollars, or implicitly through the yen-settlement spread.

When you factor these in, US high-dividend ETFs in NISA are slightly less tax-efficient than a Japanese-domiciled S&P 500 fund in the same account. If maximizing income is the goal, that's a reasonable trade. If you're optimizing total return, run the comparison first.

## Summary

- **VYM**: Best default — diversified, cheap, stable. Start here
- **HDV**: Financial quality focus, recession resilience. Good complement
- **SPYD**: Highest yield, highest volatility. For income-maximizing investors
- For long-term portfolios, **VYM is the foundation** — add SPYD or HDV with specific intent
- Understand the tax treatment in your account type before buying any of these
