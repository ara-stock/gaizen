## What's Really Scary After FIRE

When you're working toward FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early), it's natural to worry: "What if my assets run out?" But when I listen to people who have actually achieved FIRE, **money problems tend to be surprisingly minor**.

What's truly scary is **losing your connection to society**.

A workplace isn't just a source of income. It's the reason you get out of bed on a weekday morning, a place to talk with people, a space where you share the world's sense of time. Once you quit working after FIRE, your former colleagues gradually drift away in terms of schedule and values. When a friend says "the meeting this week was brutal," you're living in a world where your biggest dilemma was "what should I have for lunch yesterday."

**The feeling of falling out of society's rhythm comes quietly — but it does come, and more surely than you'd expect.**

Honestly, I don't think financial anxiety is all that significant. Here's why——

---

## The Reassurance of "I Can Work If I Need To"

The biggest mental anchor in any post-FIRE financial plan is the simple fact that you *can* work if it comes to that.

Short of an illness or disability that prevents work entirely, Japan has jobs. Even part-time work at ¥1,000–¥1,500 per hour can bring in ¥100,000–¥150,000 a month. For many people, that alone covers the bulk of living expenses.

> **There's no need to swear you'll never work again after FIRE. Retiring while keeping work as an option is what creates genuine peace of mind.**

Even if the 4% rule is designed around the assumption that your assets last 30 years, if a prolonged market crash causes your portfolio to shrink faster than expected, you can simply work a little to make up the difference. Even reducing your withdrawals in a single bad year can meaningfully extend the life of your assets over the long run.

The accumulation simulations and explanation of the 4% rule that underpin this discussion are covered in detail in my previous article, "[Accumulation Simulation for FIRE](/en/blog/fire-simulation)."

## Work as a Hobby — Convenience Stores, Uber Eats, and Resort Jobs

Working "because you have no choice" is very different from working "because you want to, because it's fun." That shift in feeling is something only FIRE can give you.

Here are some kinds of work I'm personally curious about:

| Type of Work | What Appeals to Me |
|---|---|
| **Convenience store part-time** | I've never run a register or stocked shelves — I'd like to try it once |
| **Uber Eats** | Full control over my own pace, physical activity, and perfectly fine as a one-off |
| **Resort job** | An unfamiliar environment, housing included so living costs drop, and since it's limited-term, I won't get bored |

The goal isn't to "build a career" or "sharpen skills." It's **to sharpen my understanding of worlds I haven't yet experienced**.

How does a convenience store register system actually work? What does it feel like in your body to do delivery work? There are things you can only know by doing them. And by doing them, your understanding of that world deepens by one level. That's all it is.

I believe the essence of life is living while facing your own body and mind. Achieving things is just a byproduct of that. Working as an extension of curiosity is part of that same life.

Earning even a few tens of thousands of yen a month slows the withdrawal pace. It extends the life of your assets. But more than that, I think the ability to stay curious about "things I haven't done yet" is what makes life after FIRE genuinely rich.

## The Basics of Withdrawal: Just Reverse the Accumulation

Accumulation investing was "keep buying a fixed amount every month." Withdrawal is the reverse. **Sell a fixed amount every month and use it for living expenses.**

That's all there is to it. There's no need to overcomplicate it.

To frame the symmetry between accumulation and withdrawal:

| | Accumulation Phase | Withdrawal Phase |
|---|---|---|
| **Action** | Buy a fixed amount each month | Sell a fixed amount each month |
| **Goal** | Grow your assets | Secure living expenses |
| **During a market crash** | A chance to buy cheap | Must sell more (watch out) |
| **Mindset** | Consistency is what matters | Avoiding overselling is what matters |

If you've trained yourself during the accumulation phase to "keep going even when markets fall," you can apply that same discipline to managing your mindset during withdrawal. Not panicking and selling too much when the market crashes — that's the biggest pitfall of the withdrawal phase.

---

## 定額取り崩し (Fixed-Amount Withdrawal) vs. 定率取り崩し (Fixed-Rate Withdrawal)

There are two main approaches to withdrawal.

### Fixed-Amount Withdrawal (定額取り崩し)

This means **selling a fixed dollar amount** each month or year. For example, you decide "I'll withdraw ¥200,000 per month" and stick to that plan.

**Advantages**
- Living expenses are the same every month, making household budgeting easy
- It's clear "how much I can spend this month," which keeps stress low
- Existing budgeting habits translate directly

**Disadvantages**
- When markets crash, you have to sell more units to hit the same dollar amount
- The risk of your assets shrinking significantly

### Fixed-Rate Withdrawal (定率取り崩し)

This means **withdrawing a fixed percentage of your remaining balance** each year. If you're applying the 4% rule directly, that means "sell 4% of your balance each year."

**Advantages**
- When assets shrink, the withdrawal amount automatically shrinks too (lower risk of depleting assets)
- More resilient to market crashes

**Disadvantages**
- Living expenses vary year to year, making budgeting harder
- In a year of sharp market declines, living expenses could drop significantly

### Which One to Choose?

| | Fixed-Amount | Fixed-Rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Stability of living expenses** | ◎ Same every month | △ Varies year to year |
| **Risk of depleting assets** | △ Sell more during crashes | ◎ Adjusts automatically |
| **Ease of management** | ◎ Simple | △ Requires annual calculation |
| **Best suited for** | People with high fixed costs / budget-oriented | People who can flexibly adjust spending |

Personally, I plan to go with **fixed-amount withdrawal as my base**. Being able to live with a clear sense of "this month's budget is this much" keeps me more mentally stable. As long as I have the flexibility to spend a little less and reduce my withdrawal amount in years when the market has really tumbled, that's more than enough.

## Building the "Living Within a Budget" Mindset Before FIRE

If there's one habit that matters most for life after FIRE, I think it's **the feeling of making do within a set budget**.

This isn't something you can suddenly develop after you FIRE. It's important to practice it consciously now, before you get there.

Concrete things to start doing now:

- **Set a ceiling for monthly living expenses and practice living within it** (e.g., cap living costs at ¥200,000/month and stick to it for a year)
- **Track fixed vs. variable expenses separately** (negotiate to reduce fixed costs; adjust variable costs month to month)
- **Build the habit of checking monthly whether you came in under or over budget**

> **In post-FIRE withdrawal life, your monthly withdrawal amount equals your living expense budget. The real skill that makes FIRE sustainable is the ability to live happily within that budget.**

Accumulation investing was the practice of "decide on an amount and keep going." Withdrawal is the practice of "set a budget and make it work." The direction is reversed, but the essence — **keeping at it with discipline** — is the same.

---

:::comment
Watching your balance shrink is genuinely unsettling — I won't pretend otherwise. But I've reframed it: retirees living on pension do exactly this every month, and most manage fine. The key is starting early, before FIRE, so the habit of "spending within a budget" is already ingrained.

My plan is fixed-amount withdrawal. I don't intend to draw more than I need. Automating it through a broker's periodic sale service removes the temptation to delay during downturns or accelerate when markets are up.
:::

## Summary

- **After FIRE, social isolation is scarier than money running out — maintaining connections deliberately is essential**
- **The fact that you can work if you need to is the single greatest mental safety net**
- **Working within the realm of a hobby (Uber Eats, resort jobs, etc.) is also a way to sharpen your understanding of worlds you don't yet know**
- **The basics of withdrawal are the reverse of accumulation: just sell a fixed amount each month**
- **Fixed-amount withdrawal keeps living expenses stable and is suited to household budgeting; fixed-rate withdrawal carries lower risk of depleting assets**
- **Start practicing the "living within a budget" mindset now, before you FIRE**
