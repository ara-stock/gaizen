## Why They Look Attractive

Monthly distribution funds have strong surface appeal.

- **Cash hits your account every month.** The regular deposits feel like a salary — investing becomes tangible and real
- **The yield numbers look impressive.** 5–8% annual yield figures stand out against bank deposits and bonds
- **They seem stable.** A fund that has paid monthly for years projects reliability

If you've ever considered one of these funds, you understand the appeal. But it's an illusion.

## Problem 1: Fees Are Dramatically Higher

Monthly distribution funds carry expense ratios that are often 10–20× higher than index funds.

A typical example: Invesco World Select Equity Open (Monthly Settlement Type, No Currency Hedge) charges approximately 1.6% annually. eMAXIS Slim US Stock (S&P500) charges 0.09372%. That's roughly a 17× difference.

**How that gap compounds over 10 years (¥10 million initial investment, 7% gross return assumed):**

| Expense Ratio | Net Return | After 10 Years |
|---|---|---|
| 0.09% | 6.91% | ~¥19.36M |
| 1.60% | 5.40% | ~¥16.94M |

The cost difference alone produces a gap of ~¥2.4 million over 10 years — before considering the additional load fees (2–3% upfront) that bank-sold monthly distribution funds often charge.

## Problem 2: Distribution ≠ Profit

This is the most common misunderstanding about these products.

**A portion — sometimes all — of monthly distributions is "return of capital," meaning your own money is being handed back to you.** You're not receiving investment gains. You're receiving your original investment in monthly installments.

This is sometimes called "octopus dividends" in Japan (タコ足配当) — the fund eating its own legs to survive.

In a taxable account, ordinary distributions paid from gains are generally taxed at about 20% in Japan, while return-of-capital distributions are not taxed because they reduce the investor's cost basis. Either way, cash that is withdrawn and spent no longer compounds inside the fund.

## Problem 3: Covered Calls Give Up Part of the Upside

Some monthly distribution products generate their high payouts through **covered call strategies** — selling call options on their underlying holdings and distributing the premium income as dividends.

### How It Works

The fund sells call options (the right to buy shares at a fixed price) on its holdings and collects premiums. Those premiums fund the distributions.

### Why It Underperforms

Selling a call means **capping your upside**. If the stock rises above the strike price, that gain goes to the option buyer — not you.

For example: you hold a stock at ¥100 and sell a call at ¥105. The stock rises to ¥120. You participate only up to ¥105, plus the premium you collected. Everything above ¥105 is someone else's gain.

The structural problems:

- **You systematically miss bull markets.** Every time markets run up, your returns are capped
- **The premium reinvestment cycle adds friction.** Option sale → distribution payment → investor receipt → reinvestment. The lag reduces compounding efficiency
- **Downside is not protected.** Premiums cover only a fraction of large drawdowns. You absorb the full decline, minus a small premium buffer

**Distribution rate and investment return are different figures.** QYLD's official page showed a 12.04% trailing 12-month distribution as of July 14, 2026, while its annualized NAV total return was 8.89% since inception and 10.10% over ten years through June 2026. The provider also stated that the distribution was estimated to include return of capital. A high payout must not be read as the portfolio's return.

A covered-call strategy will not underperform in every period. It tends to give up upside in a strong rally, while option premiums may help in a flat market. Compare **total return including distributions**, not the cash payout alone.

## Problem 4: Distribution Frequency Does Not Describe the Portfolio

Monthly distribution products can hold high-dividend stocks, follow a value strategy, or own the Nasdaq 100 while selling calls. The payment schedule alone does not tell you what the fund owns or how it creates cash.

Read the investment objective and benchmark, then compare total return with a lower-cost fund holding similar assets.

## Is There Ever a Case for Monthly Distribution Funds?

Rarely. The only plausible scenario:

- You are in retirement, need regular cash flow, and find managing withdrawals complex

Even then, virtually every major Japanese brokerage now offers **automatic periodic sale** (定期売却) on index funds. You can set monthly ¥XX,000 withdrawals from eMAXIS Slim S&P500 — no distribution fund needed. Same cash flow, dramatically lower costs, better tax efficiency.

That said, there are real costs. Foreign withholding tax is deducted at source, and you only recover it by filing a tax return (外国税額控除). For anyone who doesn't file, that's a permanent drag. And one honest mistake: I once bought Amazon 30-year corporate bonds without fully understanding what I was getting into. They're sitting there in the black thanks to yen depreciation, but 30 years of capital lock-up is a steep price for that result.

## Summary

I do not use a monthly distribution fund as the core of an accumulation portfolio.

- Check whether fees are higher than a lower-cost fund with similar exposure
- A distribution may include return of capital, so payout rate alone does not show profit
- Covered calls tend to cap part of the upside in a strong rally
- Compare the stated benchmark and total return including distributions

The appeal of monthly cash is real. A periodic sale from a low-cost fund may provide similar cash flow, so compare both approaches after fees and taxes.

For long-term accumulation, the main question is whether regular payouts help your goal or remove capital that could otherwise remain invested.
