## What Is the NASDAQ 100?

The NASDAQ 100 is an index of the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on the NASDAQ exchange, weighted by market capitalization.

Where the S&P 500 broadly represents the US market's 500 leading companies across all sectors, the NASDAQ 100 is a concentrated bet on technology and high-growth companies.

As of 2026, major holdings include Apple, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Amazon, Meta, Alphabet, and Tesla. Technology, communication services, and consumer discretionary dominate the sector weights.

## QQQ: The Standard Way to Access It

QQQ (Invesco QQQ Trust) is the flagship ETF tracking the NASDAQ 100. Managed by Invesco, it carries a 0.20% expense ratio and is consistently among the most-traded ETFs in the US.

For Japan-based investors, mutual fund alternatives include "eMAXIS NASDAQ100 Index" and "iFreeNEXT NASDAQ100 Index" — both of which are eligible for NISA recurring investment quotas.

## Performance vs. S&P 500

The NASDAQ 100 has outperformed the S&P 500 over most meaningful long-term periods.

| Period | NASDAQ 100 (QQQ) | S&P 500 (SPY) |
|---|---|---|
| 10-year annual return (2015–2025) | ~18–20% | ~13–15% |
| 5-year (2020–2025) | ~22–24% | ~15–17% |
| Dot-com crash (2000–2002) | ~▲83% | ~▲50% |

*These are approximate figures. Actual returns vary.*

The outperformance comes from heavy exposure to AI, semiconductors, cloud platforms, and consumer technology — all of which have been structural winners over the past decade.

## Why NASDAQ 100 Compounds Faster

### Market-cap weighting amplifies winners

Like the S&P 500, the NASDAQ 100 uses market-cap weighting. When NVIDIA grows explosively, its weight in the index grows with it, automatically concentrating the portfolio in the winning company. In a tech-led bull market, this amplification effect drives NASDAQ 100 well ahead of S&P 500.

### No financials, no energy drag

By excluding financial companies and traditional energy, NASDAQ 100 avoids sectors with lower long-term growth rates. The composition structurally favors companies with higher earnings growth potential.

## The Concentration Risk You Can't Ignore

The tradeoff is clear.

- Top 10 holdings often exceed 50% of the index
- Technology sector weight frequently exceeds 50%
- During the 2000 dot-com crash, NASDAQ 100 fell over 82%

The S&P 500 fell approximately 50% in the same period. NASDAQ 100 fell nearly twice as much. The same concentration that amplifies gains also amplifies losses.

Higher long-term returns require tolerating higher drawdowns. If a 60-80% paper loss would cause you to sell — which is the rational response many investors take — the realized outcome is much worse than the index suggests.

## How to Combine S&P 500 and NASDAQ 100

You don't have to choose. They serve different roles in a portfolio.

**A practical framework:**

- Use S&P 500 as the foundation
- Add NASDAQ 100 as a growth overlay where you have conviction

My approach is to keep S&P 500 as the core of recurring investments and use NASDAQ 100 selectively within the NISA growth investment quota. The base provides stability; the overlay captures higher growth potential in sectors I believe in.

```
Conservative     → S&P 500 only
Balanced         → S&P 500 80% + NASDAQ 100 20%
Growth-tilted    → S&P 500 50% + NASDAQ 100 50%
High-conviction  → NASDAQ 100 only
```

## NISA Account Considerations (Japan)

Several NASDAQ 100 mutual funds in Japan qualify for both the recurring investment quota and the growth investment quota in NISA — check each fund individually. One approach: fill the recurring investment quota with S&P 500, then use the growth quota to add NASDAQ 100 exposure.

## Summary

- NASDAQ 100 concentrates in the top 100 US tech and growth companies
- Long-term returns typically exceed S&P 500, but drawdowns are proportionally larger
- The 2000 crash produced an 82%+ decline — that history matters
- QQQ is the standard ETF; Japanese investors also have mutual fund alternatives
- Most practical approach: S&P 500 as the core, NASDAQ 100 as a deliberate growth overlay
- If you have conviction in technology's long-term trajectory, it belongs in your portfolio — just sized appropriately
