## The Bottom Line: Use Stocks for Long-Term Capital and Cash for Resilience

The answer to protecting wealth in an inflationary environment is simple.

**Hold assets with real demand and pricing power.** Stocks are a prime example. Cash, however, protects living expenses and known near-term spending. Do not reduce cash solely because inflation exists; separate money by when it will be used.

## Why Cash Erodes

Inflation means prices rise over time. The flip side: cash buys less and less.

When deposit rates remain below inflation, cash loses purchasing power in real terms. Cash also has no market-price volatility and protects living expenses and known near-term spending, so it should not be judged by expected return alone.

Set cash from essential spending, income stability, dependents, and known future expenses. I started my first working year with ¥500,000. See [How Much Emergency Cash Do You Need?](/en/blog/emergency-fund/) for the full framework.

## Why Stocks Should Be Your Core Asset

Companies with pricing power can pass higher input costs to customers. Not every business can do this, and stocks can still decline when inflation or interest rates rise abruptly.

Stocks may respond to inflation over long periods through pricing power and earnings growth. They are not a guaranteed hedge: higher input costs and interest rates can reduce both profits and valuations in the short term.

Concretely, holding a broadly diversified index fund tracking the S&P 500 or [All-Country](/en/blog/why-sp500-over-allcountry/) is a rational foundation. These indexes include resource, energy, and materials companies — so when commodity prices rise, their profits rise too, and you benefit indirectly without holding commodities separately.

## Evaluating Each Asset Class

### Cash

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Inflation resistance | ✕ Purchasing power erodes gradually |
| Holding framework | Emergency cash plus known spending within the next few years |

Cash is stable and immediately available. Even with a low expected return, it can prevent forced selling during a market decline.

### Stocks (Index Funds)

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Inflation resistance | ○ Companies with pricing power may adapt over time, but there is no short-term guarantee |
| Recommendation | Core asset. S&P500 or All-Country as the foundation |

Historically, equities have outpaced inflation over the long run. This is the asset class to build around.

### Gold (Regular Accumulation)

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Inflation resistance | ○ Functions as a store of value and currency hedge |
| My holding method | A small allocation through a brokerage gold accumulation plan |

Gold hedges against inflation and currency weakness. Among commodities, holding gold in a small allocation is sufficient — there is no need to diversify broadly across all commodity types.

When commodities broadly rally, the companies that extract and process them (resource and energy stocks) also rise. Your stock index already captures this indirectly.

### Commodity ETFs

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Inflation resistance | △ Theoretically useful, but… |
| Decision factors | Expense ratio, futures roll costs, and the tracked exposure |

Commodity ETFs do not always match spot prices. Depending on the product, management fees and futures roll costs can materially affect returns. Their risks also differ from shares of resource producers. I do not currently hold them, but any comparison should be made product by product.

### Physical Real Estate

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Inflation resistance | ○ Real asset with pricing power |
| Recommendation | Conditional. Requires careful preparation |

Real estate is an interesting asset class, but **understanding leverage and liquidity risk is a prerequisite.**

#### Decide the downside before buying

I prefer conservative borrowing, but an appropriate loan depends on income, vacancy, interest rates, maintenance costs, and collateral value.

More debt can increase the return on your equity, but it also increases the amount that must be covered from salary when vacancy rises or rates reset. Model whether repayments and repairs remain affordable after rental income falls.

Real estate is also **illiquid** — you often cannot sell when you want to, or at the price you want. The risk profile is fundamentally different from equities.

My own sequence is to build substantial liquid financial assets first. The decision should not rest on a single portfolio threshold: model vacancy, rising rates, repairs, taxes, and management before committing capital.

## The Deeper Point: Read the Macro Environment

The cash vs. stocks question ultimately comes down to one judgment: will inflation persist, or will deflation take hold?

In a deflationary environment, cash gains relative value. Future inflation cannot be forecast precisely, which is why a portfolio should not depend on only cash or only stocks.

Regardless of yen strength or weakness, holding globally diversified assets (S&P500, All-Country) reduces dependence on any single currency. Review your asset allocation periodically and stay aware of how the broader environment is shifting.

## Summary

- Stocks can provide inflation resilience when businesses can pass through costs, but they still carry drawdown risk
- **Use cash for emergencies and planned spending** — the amount depends on the household
- I hold a small gold allocation for currency diversification through a brokerage accumulation plan
- Commodity ETFs require product-level review of exposure and holding costs
- **Real estate requires a full cash-flow model**, including vacancy, rates, repairs, and taxes
- Separate assets by time horizon and acceptable volatility rather than relying on one inflation hedge
