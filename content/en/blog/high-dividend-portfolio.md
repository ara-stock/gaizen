## The Verdict: A Pure High-Dividend Portfolio Is Usually Unnecessary

Let me be direct. **You generally don't need to build a dedicated high-dividend portfolio.**

The total return of index funds (S&P500, All-Country) is difficult to beat by chasing dividend yield alone. Over the long term, a yield-focused approach often underperforms. That's the baseline view.

That said, **companies with both high dividends and genuine growth deserve investment consideration.** I hold some myself. "You don't need to build one" and "you shouldn't own any" are different statements.

---

## The Global Benchmark You Must Keep in Mind

Before evaluating any high-dividend stock, hold onto this reference point.

**The S&P500's long-term annualized total return (JPY-denominated, dividends reinvested) is roughly 13–18%.** This reflects the compounding profit growth of globally competitive companies.

When an individual company's profit margin is below 10%, it is — by definition — less profitable than the average global competitor. Low-margin businesses have less capacity to reinvest for growth and less ability to sustain dividend increases. **The likely outcome: total return that trails the S&P500.**

This is why "profit margin above 10%" is a non-negotiable criterion when selecting dividend stocks.

## Why High-Dividend Stocks Remain Popular

Even if the math favors index funds, dividend investing has persistent appeal — for good reasons.

**Dividends are visible cash.** Each payment is a concrete deposit you can use — for travel, for reinvestment, for whatever you want. For people who want a direct sense of their money working, dividends are satisfying in a way that index funds aren't.

Index funds only pay you when you sell. Paper gains don't pay for dinner.

This psychological edge of "seeing the cash" explains a lot of dividend investing's popularity. Just understand the tradeoff before committing.

## Defining "High Dividend"

A yield above **3.0%** is commonly called high-dividend, though there's no universal standard. I personally use **3.5%** as a rough threshold.

In recent years, rising stock prices have pushed many stocks below this level — because dividend yield falls as the stock price rises.

> **This is why I've shifted focus from "current yield" to "stocks that will deliver high yield in the future through dividend increases."**

The metric that matters is **cost yield** — the dividend relative to your purchase price — and whether that's likely to grow over time.

## Six Criteria to Check in IR Filings

When evaluating a stock, I verify five items in the investor relations materials and make one qualitative judgment.

### ✅ Five Verifiable Items

**1. Company scale**
Market capitalization matters. As discussed below, certain size ranges combined with high dividends are a warning sign.

**2. Dividend growth or non-cut policy**
Check for a track record of growing dividends or a stated commitment to not cutting them. Prior dividend cuts — and the reason for them — are important context.

**3. Payout ratio ~40%**
This is the percentage of earnings paid out as dividends. Around 40% is ideal. Above 50–60% signals limited room for future increases. A very low payout ratio can signal significant room to grow dividends ahead.

**4. Profit margin above 10%**
As explained above, this is the global competitiveness threshold. High-margin businesses can sustain both reinvestment and dividend growth simultaneously.

**5. Revenue growth 5–10%+**
Sustained dividend growth requires earnings growth. Low-growth businesses eventually exhaust their capacity to raise dividends, and a cut becomes a matter of when, not if.

### 🔍 One Qualitative Judgment

**6. Business durability**
"Will this business still exist in 10 years?" This doesn't show up in financial statements. It requires judgment. But for long-term holding, it may be the most important question.

## Why High-Dividend Companies Tend to Be Mature

Companies that pay significant dividends are typically **businesses that have largely stopped growing**.

Why? Growing companies can reinvest profits into expansion and increase enterprise value more effectively than they can by paying dividends. Once cash flows stabilize and the return on incremental investment declines, returning capital to shareholders via dividends becomes the rational choice.

### The Coca-Cola Example

Consider Coca-Cola — one of the most famous US dividend growth stocks.

Coca-Cola has global distribution and a durable brand with stable earnings. But there's a physical limit to how much soda a person can drink. Maybe one a day. Once markets mature, revenue growth slows. Paying out dividends is exactly the right thing for a company in that position.

**Put differently, investing in high-dividend companies is a choice to accept slower growth in exchange for stable cash returns.** Understanding that trade-off is essential before investing.

## Patterns to Avoid

### Mid-Cap Companies (¥50B–¥800B) Paying High Dividends

At this size, a company should still have meaningful growth potential. If it's paying high dividends anyway, it could mean **growth capital is being returned to shareholders instead** — suggesting low growth ambition or a lack of viable market expansion.

These companies tend to struggle to maintain dividends when facing market contraction or intensifying competition. **Not suitable for a long-term dividend holding strategy.**

### Payout Ratio Above 60%

A company distributing more than 60% of earnings as dividends has almost no room to increase them. Any earnings decline increases the probability of a cut. High yield that depends on an unsustainably high payout ratio is not a sustainable position.

### Sectors I Personally Avoid

**Pharmaceuticals**
High upside when drugs succeed, but success rates are low and failure is the norm. Predicting dividend sustainability is extremely difficult.

**Supermarkets and drugstore chains**
Easy to expand domestically in Japan, but difficult to restructure or pivot due to employment culture. Intense price competition compresses margins significantly.

**Telecom (mobile, internet)**
Commoditized market where price wins. Smartphone penetration is already near saturation — no meaningful growth ahead. Hard to justify expecting dividend growth.

**Finished goods manufacturers (auto, etc.)**
When product demand collapses, the business is effectively over. Auto manufacturers in particular face enormous structural switching costs — factories, equipment, and workforce — making pivots extremely difficult. The ongoing EV transition creates headwinds that are hard to assess.

**Infrastructure (utilities, gas, roads, aviation)**
Looks stable, but regulated industries face caps on profitability. High-seeming dividend yields often come with limited growth potential and low probability of meaningful increases over time.

### Japan Tobacco International / JT (2914)

JT announced a dividend cut in 2020 and the stock fell sharply. I bought during the worst of the sentiment (~¥2,200/share in 2H 2021) using the old NISA tax exemption.

My reasoning had three parts:

1. **JT stated a commitment to stable, non-cut dividends from 2021 onward**
2. **Tobacco is a discretionary habit — demand doesn't evaporate overnight despite regulation**
3. **Rumors of shareholder perks removal were driving excessive selling**

Post-COVID recovery and the new NISA's boost to dividend stock demand drove the stock up more than 3x in about five years. It is now a **"free stock" position** — I've recovered my cost basis through dividends and partial sales, and the remainder is held long-term with minimal attention.

## Summary

- **A pure high-dividend portfolio is usually unnecessary — keep the S&P500 total return as your benchmark**
- **That said, direct dividend cash flow has real lifestyle value that's worth acknowledging**
- **Focus on stocks that will become high-yield in the future via dividend growth, not current yield**
- **Always verify profit margin, revenue growth, payout ratio, and dividend track record in IR filings**
- **Avoid mid-cap stocks (¥50B–¥800B market cap) with high dividend yields**
- **Think about business durability and only hold what you can hold for the long term**
