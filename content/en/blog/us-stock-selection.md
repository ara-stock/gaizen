## Starting Point: Only Invest in What You Can Actually Understand

Individual stock investing is fundamentally different from index investing. An index captures the market average by design. Individual stocks require a judgment: "this company will grow faster than the market average."

That judgment is only reliable **within domains you actually understand.**

The reason I invest in GAFAM and semiconductors is that these sectors are close to my professional background — I can track industry developments without a significant gap between what I think is happening and what's actually happening. Invest in sectors you know, in companies with a clear growth trajectory you can articulate in your own words. That's where individual stock investing starts.

## Three Conditions for a Good Stock

### 1. High Barriers to Entry

Does the business have something competitors cannot easily replicate?

Technical complexity (patents, manufacturing know-how) and strong brand recognition act as a moat — they keep competitors out and protect profit margins over time. A company with a deep moat doesn't have to compete on price, and that means durable earnings.

### 2. High Switching Costs

Once customers start using the product or service, is it hard for them to leave?

Smartphones are the clearest example. iOS and Android operate on completely different paradigms, and switching means re-learning your device, re-buying your accessories, and leaving behind years of integrated ecosystem. Users who are satisfied have no reason to switch — and that's exactly the point.

Companies that have grown to scale have brand equity and loyal users. **Switching costs are the most powerful form of customer retention,** and they compound over time.

### 3. Recurring Purchase or Consumption

Is revenue generated once, or does the business model drive repeat sales?

Subscription models, consumables, and products that require regular upgrades produce stable, predictable revenue. These businesses are more resilient in downturns and have stronger pricing power. Recurring demand is a sign of structural durability.

Companies that satisfy all three conditions tend to **maintain competitive advantage over long periods and hold up well against inflation.**

## Choosing Sectors: Can You See Growth Continuing for 10 Years?

The test for a sector is whether you can explain in plain terms why it will still be growing a decade from now.

### GAFAM / Big Tech

Alphabet (Google), Apple, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft each satisfy the three conditions at a high level.

- **Platform dominance** (search, OS, social media, cloud)
- **Ecosystem-driven switching costs**
- **Subscription, advertising, and cloud as recurring revenue engines**

Any of the GAFAM names is a legitimate long-term investment candidate.

### Semiconductors

As AI develops, semiconductor demand is expanding across data centers, autonomous vehicles, smartphones, and industrial applications. The growth runway is clear.

**NVIDIA holds a dominant position in AI GPUs.** Part of that strength comes from the CUDA software ecosystem that developers have built on top of their hardware. As AI evolves, higher-level abstractions may reduce CUDA's lock-in, and major cloud and tech companies are developing their own custom chips. NVIDIA's current dominance is not guaranteed to last forever.

That said, NVIDIA's global manufacturing lines, existing customer base at scale, and brand are difficult to displace quickly. **Understanding both the strengths and the risks, holding NVIDIA is defensible.**

### Robotics and AI (Still Watching)

Robots assisting in healthcare and logistics will likely play a major role in the coming decades. The investment opportunity is real.

But **the industry leader is not yet clear.** Concentrating investment before a winner emerges means accepting high uncertainty. The right move is to wait until the leading company becomes evident — then you can invest with much higher conviction.

## Screening Criteria

A checklist for narrowing down individual stocks:

| Metric | Target | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| ROE | 15%+ | Is the company generating strong returns on its capital? |
| Earnings growth rate | 10%+ annualized | Is there a sustained growth trend? |
| PBR | Not excessively high | Overvaluation reduces expected returns |
| Barriers to entry | High (technology or brand) | Can competitors erode the business? |
| Revenue durability | Subscription, consumables, or required upgrades | Is the revenue structural or one-time? |
| Market cap | Large (S&P500 inclusion as a guide) | Business foundation stability and liquidity |

### Why Large-Cap Stocks

It might seem like large companies have limited room left to grow. But large-cap companies have built their business foundations, customer bases, and brands over many years — none of that is easily replicated.

**S&P500-listed stocks have high liquidity and lower risk of sudden catastrophic decline.** Smaller companies and startups offer higher upside but carry material operational, financial, and liquidity risks. For investors newer to individual stocks, starting with established large-caps is the lower-risk path.

## Summary

- **Only invest in sectors and companies you genuinely understand** — the less gap between your understanding and reality, the better your judgment
- The three conditions for a good stock: barriers to entry, switching costs, and recurring demand
- **Buy the category leader.** Second place carries meaningfully higher risk
- Any of the GAFAM names is a strong long-term investment candidate
- NVIDIA is worth holding — but understand both the competitive moat and the long-term risks
- **In sectors where the winner isn't clear yet (e.g., robotics), wait until a leader emerges before committing**
- Screening baseline: ROE 15%+, earnings growth 10%+, S&P500 inclusion as a quality filter
