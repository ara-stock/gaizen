## My Position: I Hold No Emerging Market Stocks

Let me state my stance upfront. **My portfolio contains zero emerging market stocks.**

I won't pretend these economies aren't growing. Infrastructure, banking, and energy sectors in India and Vietnam will likely expand significantly. Yet, three reasons consistently keep me from investing — and they have nothing to do with doubting the growth itself.

---

## Reason 1: Currency Moves on National Power and Popularity. The USD Is Hard to Beat

Stock returns are the product of two variables: price change and currency movement.

Even if Indian equities rise 10% in rupee terms, a 10% depreciation of the rupee against the USD wipes out the gain entirely from a dollar or yen perspective.

I think of exchange rates as a function of a country's economic strength and the market's appetite for that currency. At this point in time, I'm simply not placing an active bet on emerging market currencies sustainably outperforming the US dollar.

This isn't just theory. In 2025, the Indian rupee has continued to weaken against the USD — the USD/INR rate rose approximately 5.8% over the past six months, meaningfully eroding rupee-denominated equity gains for foreign investors.

> **If emerging market currencies begin to consistently outperform the USD, that would signal a genuine shift in global economic power — and I'd revisit the investment case.** A strengthening currency is a reflection of economic vitality, and a trend reversal would be a valid signal worth acting on.

---

## Reason 2: Information Asymmetry Is Too Large

Investing in emerging market companies means operating with far less information than I have for US or Japanese equities.

- English and Japanese analyst coverage is thin
- Corporate governance standards vary widely, and transparency is often limited
- Geopolitical and policy risks are harder to model

Investing in what I don't understand crosses into speculation. Sticking to the principle of "don't invest in what you can't understand" naturally de-prioritizes emerging markets.

---

## Reason 3: Emerging Companies Rarely Truly Threaten the S&P 500

"Companies in India and Vietnam are growing" may be true. But "therefore they'll outperform the S&P 500" doesn't follow.

For emerging market companies to capture major global market share, they'd need to displace Apple, Microsoft, or Google. To avoid that competition, most emerging companies focus on niche markets. But niche markets are, by definition, unlikely to scale globally.

The "infrastructure and banking will boom" thesis is also valid — but those gains stay local. Japanese infrastructure won't be replaced by an Indian company. American banks won't yield ground to a Vietnamese competitor. **The growth accrues within those countries' borders.** That caps the global upside.

:::comment
Honestly, India's growth story is genuinely compelling. Demographics, infrastructure demand, digital adoption — the intuition that it will keep growing is hard to argue with.

But I've come to separate "a growing country" from "a profitable investment." The people who capture most of the value from a country's growth are its citizens and domestic companies — not foreign individual investors. Currency movements eat into the gains, and thin information coverage takes another bite.

Focusing on the S&P 500 isn't a passive choice. It's an active decision to invest only in markets I can actually understand.
:::

## The Numbers: MSCI Emerging Markets vs S&P 500

| | MSCI Emerging Markets | S&P 500 |
|---|---|---|
| 10-year annualized return (2010–2019) | ~3.7% | ~13.6% |
| Long-term annualized return (since 2001) | ~7.6% | ~7.8% |
| Annualized standard deviation | ~22.4% | ~14.1% |

Over the long run, returns converge — but emerging markets carry roughly 1.6× the volatility of the S&P 500. Accepting significantly more risk for essentially the same expected return is a poor trade.

## Keeping the Antenna Up

Not investing doesn't mean not watching.

If a company from an emerging market genuinely starts displacing established players in developed markets — that's a signal worth paying attention to. It would mean a business has scaled beyond its niche and proven global competitiveness. That's when I'd take a hard look.

Similarly, if emerging market currencies begin to show a sustained trend of outperforming the USD, that would reflect a fundamental shift in global economic gravity — another trigger to reassess.

For now, I do not hold an emerging-markets index. Rather than choosing a country or index first, I limit new investments to businesses whose growth and durability I can evaluate directly.
