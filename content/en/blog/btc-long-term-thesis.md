## How I Position BTC

I treat BTC not as equity, but as a **commodity** — in the same category as watches, wine, or art: scarce physical (or digital) goods whose price is driven by supply and demand rather than cashflows.

There is no "enterprise value" or "cash flow" in the traditional sense. But as a commodity, **price is determined by the balance of supply and demand**.

> This article focuses on BTC's role and sizing within my portfolio. For the broader investment thesis, including scarcity and network effects, see "[My Bitcoin Investment Thesis](/en/blog/btc-investment-thesis/)."

## Why I Hold It

### 1. Hard Supply Cap and Natural Supply Reduction

BTC's current consensus rules set a 21 million coin limit. Changing those rules would require broad agreement among network participants. More importantly, **the amount actually in circulation is less than the cap**.

- Private keys (wallet passwords) permanently lost
- Coins sent to wrong addresses
- Early-mined coins that have never moved

This resembles gold lying buried in mountains or the ocean floor — but with one critical difference: **lost BTC cannot be recovered. Ever.** Coins that disappear from circulation never return. Over the long term, effective supply continuously decreases.

### 2. Core User Conviction

Among investors, a core group treats BTC as "digital gold" or a "currency independent of nation-states" — and holds with conviction. These passionate holders don't sell when prices fall, further reducing liquid supply.

Even with stable demand, constrained supply drives prices higher — that's the basic price mechanism for BTC as a commodity.

### 3. Inflation and Geopolitical Hedge

Fiat currencies (yen, dollar, etc.) are controlled by central banks. Large-scale monetary easing and fiscal spending dilute currency value. BTC has no issuer, so it is immune to this dilution.

It is sometimes valued as an asset that does not depend on one government or financial institution. However, BTC can also fall sharply during crises, so its effectiveness as a geopolitical hedge is not guaranteed.

### 4. Evolution as a Payment Method

BTC is increasingly usable as a **daily payment method** via crypto debit cards. At the point of payment, BTC is converted to stablecoins (USDC, USDT) at the current market rate and settled. BTC is becoming not just a "hold and wait" asset, but one that is actually used.

## Comparison with Gold

| Item | Gold | BTC |
|---|---|---|
| Supply | Continuously increases via mining | Hard cap 21M; effective supply decreases via losses |
| Lost assets | Physically recoverable (potentially) | Permanently unrecoverable |
| Divisibility | Low | Very high (satoshi units) |
| Transferability | Difficult | Instant global transfer |
| Storage cost | High (vault, transport) | Wallet-only |
| Payment use | Not practical | Possible via crypto card |

## Portfolio Role and Allocation

BTC's role in my portfolio is **inflation hedge, geopolitical hedge, and diversification**. Given the following risks, I limit allocation to a **maximum of 5%**.

**Why I keep allocation small:**

- **Hack risk** — Exchange and wallet attacks are real. Even self-custody (cold wallet) carries operational risk
- **Pricing opacity** — As a commodity, there is no clear valuation anchor like earnings or dividends. Price appreciation beyond inflation (speculation, mania) is difficult to quantify
- **Regulatory risk** — Policy shifts in major jurisdictions could significantly affect liquidity and price

## Lending BTC as a Long-Term Holder

If you're holding BTC for the long term, depositing it in a lending market is one option. You can pledge BTC as collateral to borrow stablecoins (USDC, USDT), then deploy those stablecoins in DeFi yield farming or other strategies.

If you have long-term BTC sitting idle, there is potential to earn yield while continuing to hold.

However, **hacking risk is always present**. Protocol vulnerabilities, smart contract bugs, exchange failures, and lending platform collapses — all of these have happened before. Carefully size any lending position only after fully understanding the risk that deposited assets may not be returned.

## Summary

BTC doesn't generate cash flows the way equities do via earnings or dividends. However, through lending markets or using BTC as collateral to generate yield on borrowed stablecoins, indirect cash flows are possible.

**Supply reduction, core user conviction, inflation hedging, evolution as payment infrastructure, and yield via Lend/Borrow** — taken together, I believe BTC has a meaningful place in a diversified portfolio.

My approach: hold long-term, maintain a maximum 5% allocation.
