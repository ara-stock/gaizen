## Choose a Fund From Its Documents, Not Its Name

Japanese mutual fund names often include attractive terms such as "growth," "selected," "global," or "high dividend." The name does not tell you what the fund actually owns, how much it costs, or how much risk it takes.

Review three main documents before investing:

| Document | Main Role | What to Check |
|---|---|---|
| Prospectus | Explains the product design | Investments, risks, purchase fees, expense ratio, and redemption conditions |
| Monthly report | Shows recent portfolio status | Assets under management, holdings, allocation, and recent returns |
| Investment report | Reports actual operations | Actual costs, management results, and benchmark differences |

You do not need to read every page in detail. Beginners can define a short list of important checks and eliminate unsuitable candidates in order.

:::comment
When I select a mutual fund, the first things I review are its holdings, assets under management, and fees.

I mainly own S&P500 and All-Country funds. Rather than constantly searching for new products, I compare a candidate with S&P500 and ask whether there is a clear reason to add it. Many products look sophisticated, but their total return and costs often leave me preferring the simpler S&P500 option.
:::

## The First Seven Checks

I review funds in this order:

1. Investment target and holdings
2. Assets under management and fund flows
3. Purchase fee
4. Expense ratio
5. Actual cost
6. Total return
7. Sharpe ratio

The first five checks explain the product and its continuing cost. The final two help assess whether its return and risk justify owning it.

## 1. Investment Target and Holdings

First, determine what you will actually own after buying the fund.

The prospectus explains the investment policy and tracked index. The monthly report usually shows top holdings and allocation by country or sector.

### Questions to Ask

- Which index, asset, or region does the fund target?
- What are the top ten holdings?
- Is concentration in the largest holdings too high?
- Does it overlap with funds you already own?
- Does the actual portfolio match what the product name suggests?

A global equity fund, for example, may still hold a majority of its assets in US stocks. Buying multiple funds may not create as much diversification as expected if their largest holdings are the same.

## 2. Assets Under Management and Fund Flows

Assets under management, or AUM, represents the total value managed inside the fund. It is usually available in the monthly report and on the asset manager's product page.

Large funds generally have several potential advantages:

- Greater likelihood of continuing operations
- Fixed costs spread across more investors
- Possible room for future expense-ratio reductions
- Easier visibility into whether money continues flowing into the fund

For small funds with continuing outflows, review the possibility of early redemption and the efficiency of operations. A small AUM figure does not automatically mean a fund will close. Early redemption conditions are stated in the prospectus.

:::comment
My own standard is deliberately conservative: for a core long-term holding, I generally choose funds with at least ¥500 billion in AUM.

That does not mean a fund below ¥50 billion is automatically dangerous. I simply prefer a large fund with continuing inflows when I expect to contribute for many years.
:::

### Review the Direction, Not Only the Current Amount

Use monthly reports to inspect the trend:

- AUM rising alongside the fund's price
- Units increasing through new investor contributions
- AUM declining continuously

A fund can remain large while experiencing persistent outflows. The direction matters alongside the current size.

## 3. Purchase Fee

The purchase fee is deducted when buying a mutual fund.

If a fund charges a 1% purchase fee, investing ¥1 million creates approximately ¥10,000 in cost before management begins. If returns are otherwise equal, it starts behind a no-load fund.

As a general rule, I select funds with **no purchase fee**.

Many low-cost index funds in Japan are no-load. When a purchase fee applies, confirm that the product offers enough value to justify paying it.

## 4. Expense Ratio

The expense ratio is continuously deducted from fund assets while you hold the product.

Unlike a one-time purchase fee, it affects returns every year. Small differences accumulate over a long holding period.

:::comment
My acceptable expense-ratio range is generally around 0.1% to 0.2% annually. Above that level, I examine carefully whether the investment exposure or management results justify the cost.

Higher cost directly reduces investor return. When two funds track the same index, I prioritize the lower-cost option.
:::

A low expense ratio alone does not guarantee a good fund. However, when comparing products with the same investment target and index, cost is one of the clearest differences.

## 5. Actual Cost

Do not stop after reading the stated expense ratio. **Always check the actual cost.**

Actual cost includes the expense ratio plus costs incurred during operations, such as trading commissions, custody expenses, and audit fees. In Japanese investment reports, it is often shown in a section similar to "expenses per 10,000 units."

### What to Review

- Whether costs beyond the expense ratio are significant
- Whether costs increased from the previous period
- Whether actual cost is high relative to funds tracking the same index
- Whether overseas custody or transaction expenses are unusually large

New funds may not have an investment report showing actual cost yet. In that case, review similar products from the same manager or wait until operating results are available.

## 6. Total Return

Total return combines changes in fund value with distributions paid.

Looking only at distribution yield can make a fund appear profitable even when it returns investor capital or its NAV declines. Compare mutual funds using total return rather than distributions alone.

When reviewing a candidate, I compare it with an S&P500 fund over the same period.

### Comparison Rules

- Use the same start and end dates
- Align currency conditions
- Include reinvested distributions
- Review three- and five-year periods when available, not only one year
- Remember that past return does not guarantee future results

Funds have different launch dates, so comparing "return since inception" can be misleading.

## 7. Sharpe Ratio

The Sharpe ratio measures return relative to price volatility. A higher figure indicates that a fund produced more return per unit of historical risk.

When considering a fund beyond S&P500, I check whether its total return and Sharpe ratio compare favorably with S&P500.

The Sharpe ratio changes with the measurement period. Do not compare values calculated over different dates or using different assumptions. A high historical Sharpe ratio also does not guarantee future returns or downside protection.

You can use this site's "[Sharpe Ratio Comparison Tool](/en/tools/sharpe/)" to compare price movements over a common period.

## How Much Benchmark Difference Matters?

An index fund aims to track a target index, but fees, trading costs, dividend taxes, and fund flows prevent perfectly identical results.

Investigate when the difference from the benchmark remains consistently large.

I do not focus on small differences when a fund has low cost, large AUM, and broadly tracks its index over time. Minor figures alone are not a reason to switch repeatedly.

## Products Beginners Commonly Misunderstand

### Monthly Distribution Funds

Monthly distribution funds can look highly profitable because cash arrives every month. However, distributions may include returned investor capital. Review NAV and total return together.

See "[Why You Should Avoid Monthly Distribution Investment Funds](/en/blog/monthly-distribution-fund-warning/)" for details.

### Funds With High Purchase Fees

A fund with a purchase fee above 1% begins with an immediate loss equal to that fee. When low-cost alternatives exist, the cost difference affects long-term results.

### Products That Look Better Because They Are Complex

Thematic, leveraged, and monthly distribution funds may appear more advanced because their structures are complex. Complexity does not guarantee higher returns.

Compare total return and Sharpe ratio with S&P500 over the same period, then decide whether the additional cost and risk produced enough value.

## A Five-Minute Review Process

When time is limited, review candidates in this order:

```
1. Monthly report: holdings and AUM
2. Prospectus: investment target, purchase fee, and expense ratio
3. Investment report: actual cost
4. Monthly report or comparison data: total return over the same period
5. Sharpe ratio over the same period
6. Decide whether there is a reason to own it instead of S&P500 or a similar fund
```

Do not decide from one metric. Review cost, portfolio content, and actual results together.

## Summary

- Use the prospectus to review investments, risk, purchase fees, and expense ratio
- Use the monthly report to review AUM, fund flows, and holdings
- Use the investment report to check actual cost, not only the stated expense ratio
- My conservative standard is no purchase fee, roughly 0.1%–0.2% expense ratio, and at least ¥500 billion in AUM
- AUM alone does not determine early redemption; review the conditions in the prospectus
- Compare total return and Sharpe ratio with S&P500 over the same dates
- Judge monthly distribution funds and high-fee products by total results, not headline yield

:::comment
The large number of mutual funds can make investors feel that there must be one perfect product to discover. I use S&P500 or All-Country as the foundation and require a clear reason before adding anything else.

Always check actual cost. A low advertised expense ratio can still differ from the total expenses incurred in operation. Mutual funds are designed to be held for years, so a few minutes of review before purchasing can matter over the long term.
:::
