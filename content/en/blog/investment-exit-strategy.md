## Exit Strategy Isn't Just for FIRE Seekers

The term "exit strategy" calls to mind people pursuing early retirement. But **every investor eventually reaches a phase where assets need to become income.**

Working hours change. Income decreases. Major expenses arrive. If you haven't thought through how to handle those transitions, decisions get emotional — and emotional investment decisions are usually the expensive kind.

An exit strategy isn't a plan for ending. It's a plan for how you want to live.

## Two Axes to Think About

### 1. Investment exit: when and how to draw down assets

Converting accumulated assets into living expenses, spending money, and income.

### 2. Work exit: when and how to change your working arrangement

How long to stay in full-time employment. When to consider freelancing, part-time work, or a reduced schedule. This decision is directly tied to your asset level.

These two axes connect. Your investment exit plan shapes your work exit options, and vice versa.

## The 4% Rule as a Starting Point

The "4% rule" has two foundational sources: William Bengen's 1994 paper "Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data" (Journal of Financial Planning), and the 1998 study "Retirement Savings: Choosing a Withdrawal Rate That Is Sustainable" by Cooley, Hubbard, and Walz — commonly called the Trinity Study. Both conclude that withdrawing 4% of your portfolio annually gives the portfolio a high probability of lasting 30+ years, assuming a stock/bond mix typical of US markets.

If your annual living expenses are ¥3,000,000, you'd need ¥75,000,000 to apply the 4% rule.

The 4% rule has assumptions:
- US stock and bond allocation
- 30-year time horizon
- Pre-tax figures — transaction costs and taxes aren't factored in

For Japanese investors, accounting for 20.315% capital gains tax on withdrawals and the possibility of a longer time horizon than 30 years, a 3–3.5% withdrawal rate may be more conservative and sustainable.

## Fixed Amount vs. Fixed Percentage Withdrawals

**Fixed percentage (withdraw X% of remaining portfolio each year):**
Withdrawal amount naturally shrinks in bad years and grows in good years. Lower risk of depleting the portfolio, but income is variable. Harder to budget around.

**Fixed amount (withdraw ¥XX,000 per month regardless of market):**
Consistent income that's easy to plan around. In down markets, you sell more units to hit the target — which can accelerate depletion if the market stays down.

Neither is universally superior. Most practical approaches **combine both** — a base fixed amount for essential expenses, with flexibility to adjust in strong or weak market years.

## Use Automatic Periodic Sale to Remove Emotion

Both Rakuten Securities and SBI Securities offer automatic periodic sale (定期売却) for investment trusts. You set the amount or percentage, and the brokerage handles it monthly.

This removes the "should I sell now?" decision from the equation. You don't need to time withdrawals any more than you needed to time purchases during the accumulation phase. The same system applies.

NISA accounts support periodic sale as well — the tax-exempt status extends through the withdrawal phase.

## Gradual Transitions Are More Common Than Hard Stops

The image of "going full FIRE one day" is less common in practice than a gradual shift in working arrangements.

A realistic progression:

1. **Full-time employment** — primary accumulation phase
2. **Add side income or investment income as a supplement** — reduce dependence on salary
3. **Reduce work to part-time or freelance** — investment income covers the gap
4. **Optional: full FIRE** — labor income at zero, fully asset-funded

Most people find a comfortable stopping point somewhere in the middle of this range. The key is being able to evaluate each transition numerically: does my current asset level support this next step?

## Questions to Clarify Your Exit Shape

Rather than aiming for a single universal answer, these questions help define what exit looks like for you:

- **What standard of living do I want to maintain?** How much per month is "enough"? Include variable costs: travel, hobbies, education, unexpected expenses
- **Do I want to stop working, or do I want the freedom to choose?** If you enjoy your work, "exit" means something different — it's about optionality, not stopping
- **How much do I want to leave behind?** Spending everything down versus leaving assets for family or causes changes the required portfolio size significantly
- **How am I factoring in healthcare and elderly care costs?** Japanese elderly care costs are significant and often underestimated in early financial plans

Answering these honestly gives you a clearer picture of the exit you're actually designing for — not a generic model.

## Summary

- Exit strategy applies to every investor, not just FIRE seekers
- Think about investment exit (asset drawdown) and work exit (career transitions) together
- The 4% rule is a useful benchmark; adjust to 3–3.5% for Japanese tax context and longer time horizons
- Automatic periodic sale removes timing decisions from the withdrawal phase
- A gradual transition from full-time to part-time to fully asset-funded is often more practical than a hard FIRE date
- Define what you actually want from life before optimizing for a number
