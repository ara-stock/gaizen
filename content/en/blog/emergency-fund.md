## Start with the shortfall if your income stops

An emergency fund is cash for an unexpected expense or loss of income. Three or six months of expenses can be useful starting points, but neither is a universal answer. The amount depends on essential spending, income stability, dependents, and the public benefits available to you.

When I restarted investing in my first year of full-time work, I first kept **¥500,000 in cash**. I chose it after considering my monthly obligations, stable salary, and whether family support was available. It was the amount that let me continue investing without worrying about the next unexpected bill.

:::comment
Emergency cash is not intended to maximize returns. Its job is to keep you from selling investments to pay bills during a market decline. I personally hold relatively little cash today, but I would not use my current risk level as a universal recommendation for beginners.
:::

## Four questions for setting a target

### 1. What are your essential monthly expenses?

Add the costs that continue even after income stops: housing, utilities, food, insurance, tax, debt payments, and essential family expenses. Separate travel and hobbies that can be paused.

If essential spending is ¥150,000 a month, three months is ¥450,000 and six months is ¥900,000. This calculation is more useful than applying a fixed multiple to your normal lifestyle spending.

### 2. How stable is your income?

A salaried employee, a worker with a large variable-pay component, and a freelancer face different risks. A household that can cover essentials with either partner’s income also differs from one dependent on a single earner.

Freelancers and business owners generally need a larger buffer because revenue can fluctuate and employee benefits may not apply.

### 3. Which public benefits apply, and when are they paid?

In Japan, eligible employees who cannot work because of a non-work illness or injury may receive sickness allowance. It is generally based on two-thirds of standard daily remuneration and can be paid for a cumulative period of up to 18 months.

Eligibility, waiting periods, paperwork, and payment timing still matter. Unemployment benefits also vary by employment history and reason for leaving. **A future benefit is not the same as cash available for this month’s bills.** Keep enough liquidity to bridge the period before payments arrive.

### 4. Do you have a known large expense ahead?

Moving, a vehicle inspection, childbirth, education, or a home purchase should be funded separately. Money needed within the next few years should not depend on stock prices because the market may be down when the bill arrives.

## Can investments replace an emergency fund?

I keep the option of selling part of my portfolio when necessary, but investments are not a complete substitute for cash.

Job losses and recessions can occur at the same time as market declines. Being forced to sell at a loss while income has stopped is precisely what emergency cash helps prevent. Settlement time and taxes outside NISA also matter.

For that reason, the core emergency fund belongs in an immediately accessible, principal-stable account such as an ordinary bank deposit.

## Must you wait before investing?

You do not have to choose between saving six months of cash and doing nothing. If the household budget is positive and near-term bills are covered, you can build cash while starting a ¥1,000 monthly NISA contribution.

1. Cover bills through the next payday and small unexpected expenses
2. Start with an amount that cannot disrupt daily life
3. Prioritize cash until your emergency target is reached
4. Increase the recurring investment gradually afterward

The purpose of a small investment is to learn the account and experience market movement, not to sacrifice financial resilience. If you have expensive debt, a monthly deficit, or a known near-term expense, repair the budget first.

## Separate account or one account?

A dedicated account can prevent accidental spending. A single account is simpler to manage. I track bank and brokerage balances together in a monthly spreadsheet, so I can identify emergency cash without adding another account.

The important point is not the number of accounts. You need to know how much emergency cash exists and keep it distinct from ordinary spending money.

## My risk level is not the reader’s risk level

Today I keep relatively little cash because I have recurring employment income and liquid investments. I am also comfortable investing more when markets fall.

That decision reflects my employment, expenses, family situation, and tolerance for risk. If the possibility of selling during a decline would cause anxiety, holding more cash may make it easier to stay invested. The purpose of emergency cash is not to improve expected returns; it is to make the long-term plan durable.

## Summary

- Set emergency cash from essential expenses and the shortfall after income stops
- Employment, dependents, public benefits, and planned spending change the target
- Account for benefit eligibility and the time before money arrives
- Investments can fall when you need cash and are not a complete substitute
- Building cash and starting a ¥1,000 contribution can happen in parallel
- I restarted investing after first setting aside ¥500,000
