## Why the "6-Month Rule" Exists

"You should keep six months of living expenses set aside as an emergency fund" — this piece of personal finance advice has spread widely and become a staple recommendation.

But why six months? The rationale is surprisingly vague: the idea is to **prepare for sudden unemployment, illness, or accident so that you can survive for half a year even if your income stops completely.** If your monthly expenses are ¥200,000, that means keeping ¥1,200,000 sitting in a bank account; at ¥300,000 a month, it means ¥1,800,000.

The underlying concept is not wrong. But once it gets interpreted as "you must not start investing until you've saved up a full six months' worth," the story changes. **That is an opportunity cost.**

---

## My Answer: 1 Month (Plus a Buffer) Is Enough

My view is simple.

**Setting aside 1 to 1.5 months of your monthly obligations as an emergency fund is sufficient.**

> If your monthly obligations are ¥100,000, keep around ¥150,000 in your bank account. Everything else goes into investments.

Why is that enough? Two reasons:

1. **For a genuine emergency (illness, unemployment), you can simply sell part of your stock holdings.**
2. **Every year you spend "not investing" while young becomes irreversible opportunity cost.**

| Approach | Amount held as cash (assuming ¥200,000/month) | Portion available to invest |
|---|---|---|
| Save 6 months first, then invest | ¥1,200,000 held in cash | Low (takes a long time to accumulate) |
| Start investing with 1–1.5 months saved | ¥200,000–¥300,000 held in cash | **High (the rest goes to work immediately)** |

My personal belief is that when you're young, you should be comfortable putting **everything** — beyond basic living expenses and a small cash buffer — into investments.

## The Illness and Injury Scenario: You Can Just Sell Some Stocks

"But what if I get sick?" — that fear is the root of the "six-month rule" mentality. Let's work through what actually happens.

### For Company Employees: 傷病手当金 (Sick Leave Allowance / 傷病手当金) Exists

If a company employee enrolled in social insurance becomes unable to work due to illness or injury, they are entitled to receive **傷病手当金 (sick leave allowance / 傷病手当金)**.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Benefit amount | Average standard monthly remuneration over the last 12 months ÷ 30 days × 2/3 |
| Benefit period | Up to 1 year and 6 months (since 2022, counted cumulatively) |
| Where to apply | Your enrolled 健康保険組合 (employer health insurance society) or 協会けんぽ (Japan's national health insurance association / 協会けんぽ) |

On a monthly salary of ¥300,000, sick leave allowance pays a maximum of roughly ¥200,000 per month for up to a year and a half. **That is a powerful safety net.**

If you're receiving sick leave allowance and simply sell stock to cover any shortfall, there is no need to keep six months of cash sitting idle.

> **Note: 傷病手当金 is only available to company employees enrolled in social insurance.** Freelancers and sole proprietors are covered by 国民健康保険 (national health insurance) and cannot access this benefit. If you are self-employed, you need to design your finances to keep a larger cash cushion on hand.

### Selling Stocks Is Not a "Last Resort"

Some people worry that selling stocks means locking in a loss. But if you've been investing long-term, there's a strong chance your holdings have grown well beyond your original principal after several years. **Selling what you need, when you need it — that is also a valid use of investing.**

Meanwhile, cash sitting idle is subject to real value erosion through inflation. Don't overlook the risk of doing nothing.

## Should You Keep a Separate Account?

You often see the advice "keep your emergency fund in a dedicated account." I did exactly that when I first started working — I opened a separate account and managed it there.

**Speaking from that experience, I personally think a single account is fine.**

### Pros and Cons of a Separate Account

| | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Separate account** | Psychologically distinct as "money you don't touch" | More accounts to check; managing multiple passwords becomes a hassle |
| **Single account** | Simpler to manage | The boundary between "money I can spend" and "money I shouldn't" can blur |

Keeping a separate account is not a bad idea in itself. If having a dedicated account helps you commit to not touching those funds, it can be effective.

That said, more accounts mean more friction — checking balances, managing logins, transfer fees for moving money around. **What matters is not the number of accounts, but staying in a position where your money can be put to work in investments quickly.**

If you have a clear picture of your cash flows between living expenses and investment funds, a single account works perfectly well.

## Conclusion for Young Investors: Be Aware of Opportunity Cost

What happens when someone in their 20s or 30s decides "I won't invest until I've saved six months of expenses"?

Say someone with ¥150,000 in monthly obligations decides to "wait until they have ¥900,000." Even if they save ¥50,000 every month, that takes 18 months. **They end up waiting on the sidelines for a year and a half.**

Applying the S&P 500's historical average annual return (roughly 7–10%), the opportunity cost of that year and a half is far from trivial. The power of compounding grows larger the sooner you start, and it cannot be recaptured later.

**It is often said that one year of investing while young is worth five years of investing in your 50s.**

Rather than spending time debating the size of your emergency fund, the far more important move is simply to **start**.

## Summary

- **An emergency fund of 1–1.5 months of obligations is sufficient** — you don't need the full "6-month rule" (e.g., if your obligations are ¥100,000/month, ~¥150,000 is enough)
- **Company employees have a powerful safety net in 傷病手当金 (sick leave allowance)**. Cover any gap by selling a portion of your holdings
- **Freelancers and sole proprietors are not eligible for 傷病手当金** — consider keeping a larger cash reserve
- **What matters more than how many accounts you have is staying ready to put money into investments at any time**
- **When you're young, put everything beyond your emergency fund into investments to minimize opportunity cost**
