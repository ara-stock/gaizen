## The Short Answer: Start With S&P500 or All-Country, Then Choose What You Understand

If you are choosing your first mutual fund for NISA, I would start with two broad candidates: an **S&P500 index fund** and an **All-Country global equity index fund**.

I do not think one is the only correct answer. Both can work as a core long-term investment. In fact, I personally invest in both.

The important point is not to buy because someone recommended it. Investing is a repeated process of researching, understanding, and making your own decision. Choose the fund that you can explain and continue holding.

:::comment
Many beginners get stuck choosing between S&P500 and All-Country. For long-term accumulation, I do not expect the difference in outcome to be dramatically large.

The right choice depends on what you care about most: expense ratio, total return, assets under management, geographic diversification, or your confidence in US companies. I think both are strong options.
:::

## Look at the Fund, Not Just the Brokerage

You can buy major low-cost mutual funds through both Rakuten Securities and SBI Securities.

Each brokerage has its own point programs and proprietary funds, but beginners should first focus on the **fund itself**.

Check the following:

- What does the fund invest in?
- Which index does it track?
- What is the expense ratio?
- Is the fund large enough?
- Are assets still flowing into the fund?
- What is the actual cost shown in the investment report?
- Can you keep holding it during a market decline?

Brokerage campaigns and point rewards are secondary. A mutual fund is something you may hold for years, so fund design and cost matter more than the account provider.

## NISA Mutual Fund Checklist

Before buying a fund in NISA, check these items.

| Item | Why It Matters | My Rule of Thumb |
|---|---|---|
| Investment target | Understand what you actually own | S&P500 or global equities as core candidates |
| Purchase fee | Avoid starting with an immediate cost | No-load funds only |
| Expense ratio | Ongoing costs reduce long-term returns | Prefer below 0.1% for core holdings |
| Actual cost | The stated expense ratio is not the whole picture | Check the investment report |
| Assets under management | Scale supports continuity and efficiency | Prefer JPY 500 billion or more |
| Total return | Avoid judging by distributions alone | Compare over the same period |
| Ability to keep holding | Prevent panic selling | Buy only what you can explain |

This checklist narrows the field quickly. Beginners do not need to compare dozens of funds.

## How to Choose Between S&P500 and All-Country

The rough difference is simple.

| Fund Type | Feature | Suitable For |
|---|---|---|
| S&P500 | Focused on large US companies | Investors who believe in the long-term strength of US companies |
| All-Country | Global equity exposure, still heavily weighted toward the US | Investors who prefer broader geographic diversification |

All-Country does not mean equal exposure to every country. Because it is usually market-cap weighted, US stocks still make up a large portion. The top holdings often overlap heavily with S&P500 funds.

That is why I do not think either choice is a fatal mistake.

### Ask What You Care About

If you are unsure, ask:

- Do I want stronger exposure to US companies?
- Do I prefer the comfort of global diversification?
- How much do I care about the expense ratio?
- Am I looking at total return over the same period?
- Can I keep buying this fund during a drawdown?

You do not have to treat this as an all-or-nothing decision. Holding both is also possible. Just understand that it increases overlap in US stocks.

For more detail, see "[Why I Hold Both S&P500 and All-Country Funds](/en/blog/why-sp500-over-allcountry/)".

## Funds Beginners Should Avoid

NISA is best used for long-term holdings. Funds designed for short-term themes or high visible payouts are usually not ideal as core holdings.

I would avoid the following types as a beginner.

## 1. Monthly Distribution Funds

Monthly distribution funds can feel attractive because they pay cash regularly. But some distributions may include a return of principal, meaning you are partly receiving your own money back.

For long-term wealth building, check total return instead of focusing on the distribution amount.

See "[Why You Should Avoid Monthly Distribution Funds](/en/blog/monthly-distribution-fund-warning/)" for more detail.

## 2. Funds With Purchase Fees

Funds with purchase fees start at a disadvantage. If a fund charges a purchase fee, you need a strong reason to accept that cost.

Today, many low-cost index funds are no-load. If you cannot explain why a purchase fee is worth paying, avoid it.

## 3. Funds With High Expense Ratios

The expense ratio is deducted for as long as you hold the fund.

For core NISA holdings, I generally prefer funds with an expense ratio below 0.1%. If a fund exceeds that level, I want a very clear reason for paying the extra cost.

Because major S&P500 and All-Country funds have become very low cost, beginners should usually start by comparing those options first.

## 4. Small Funds

Small funds require extra caution. They may be less efficient to operate, and in some cases they may face early redemption risk.

My conservative rule is to prefer funds with at least JPY 500 billion in assets under management. Smaller funds are not automatically bad, but for a core long-term NISA holding, scale provides comfort.

## 5. Thematic Funds

Thematic funds focus on currently popular themes such as AI, semiconductors, India, space, or decarbonization.

The theme may be genuinely attractive. But today's popular theme may not remain popular six months or one year later.

If you have strong conviction that the trend will last, you can research it. But for a beginner's core NISA holding, I would prioritize broad, low-cost index funds over thematic products.

## A Simple Decision Process

If you are stuck, use this order.

1. Decide your monthly contribution amount
2. Start with S&P500 or All-Country as candidates
3. Confirm there is no purchase fee
4. Check the expense ratio and actual cost
5. Check assets under management and asset flows
6. Compare total return over the same period
7. Choose the fund you can keep holding

If you still cannot decide, splitting between S&P500 and All-Country is a reasonable option. You can learn while investing.

Just do not put a large amount into a fund you do not understand. Starting small is enough.

## Summary

- S&P500 and All-Country are both strong candidates for a core NISA mutual fund
- The long-term result may not be dramatically different
- Focus on the fund, not only the brokerage
- Prefer no purchase fee, low expense ratio, sufficient scale, and transparent actual costs
- Avoid monthly distribution funds, purchase fees, high costs, small funds, and thematic funds as core holdings
- The best fund is one you understand and can keep holding

:::comment
The goal is not to find a perfect answer. The goal is to choose a fund you can keep buying without panic.

For beginners, a low-cost, diversified S&P500 or All-Country fund is already a strong start. What matters after buying is not selling just because the market falls.
:::
