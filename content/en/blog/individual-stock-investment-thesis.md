## Conclusion: a thesis is a reviewable decision record, not a perfect forecast

Investing in an individual company does not require a perfect share-price forecast. It requires a clear explanation of why I am buying, what I need to keep monitoring, and what would make me reconsider.

That is what I mean by an investment thesis. My actual research sequence is:

**Market capitalization → business → financial results → product or service → share price, P/E, ROE, and other indicators**

I deliberately leave the share price until last. This helps reduce the temptation to call a company good because its stock is rising or cheap because its stock has fallen. I first decide whether the business deserves to be held for years.

| Stage | What I examine | One sentence to record |
|---|---|---|
| Market cap | Company scale and the boundaries of my investable universe | Why this size fits my process |
| Business | Customers, revenue sources, and durability of demand | How the company makes money |
| Results | Revenue, profit, cash flow, and company-specific metrics | Whether the thesis appears in the numbers |
| Product | Usability, pricing, and differentiation | Why customers should keep choosing it |
| Valuation | P/E, ROE, history, and peer comparison | Whether the price assumes too much growth |

---

## 1. Start with market capitalization

I check market capitalization first. I prefer owning established large companies with proven demand, customers, brands, and infrastructure rather than seeking maximum upside from small companies. I also tend to allocate more capital to companies with larger market values.

Market cap is still only market price multiplied by shares outstanding. It does not directly measure safety or business quality. A large company can suffer a major decline because of weaker earnings, regulation, or a technology transition. Market cap is a screening tool, not an investment thesis.

I use it to ask:

- Is the company within a scale and business area I can understand?
- Is there sufficient liquidity when I need to transact?
- Does the exposure overlap with stocks already held through funds or other positions?

:::comment
I consciously invest more in larger companies. Reaching scale usually reflects underlying demand, brand value, or a business platform that took time to build.

I do not buy on size alone. Market cap narrows the field before I research the business and results.
:::

---

## 2. Explain the business in one sentence

Next, I identify who pays the company and what they pay for. Listing segment names is not enough; I want one sentence explaining how profit is generated.

For Alphabet, that sentence is: “Search and YouTube attract users and advertisers, and the resulting cash funds Cloud and AI.” For MUFG: “Domestic lending provides a base while overseas operations and fee businesses diversify earnings.”

I examine more than current revenue:

- why customers continue to use the product or service;
- whether margins can withstand higher costs;
- which infrastructure, technology, brands, or customer relationships competitors cannot quickly reproduce; and
- why demand should still exist five years from now.

If I cannot explain the business simply, I will struggle to recognize a meaningful change after buying it.

---

## 3. Convert the story into financial evidence

Once I understand the business, I test it against financial results. For a US-listed company, Forms 10-K and 10-Q contain descriptions of the business, risks, financial statements, and management's discussion of performance. For Japanese companies, I use earnings releases, presentation materials, and annual securities reports.

At minimum, I check:

1. Year-on-year changes in revenue and operating profit.
2. Whether operating margin improved and why it deteriorated if it did.
3. The relationship between operating cash flow and capital expenditure.
4. Management's outlook and the assumptions behind it.
5. Metrics specific to the thesis.

Those specific metrics differ by company. For Alphabet, I monitor YouTube advertising, Google Cloud revenue and operating profit, and capital expenditure. For MUFG, I monitor lending spreads, net interest income, credit costs, and the CET1 ratio. The right question is not which generic metric applies to every company, but where the purchase rationale should appear in the accounts.

---

## 4. Use the product to detect change before it reaches the accounts

When possible, I use the product or service myself. I consider usability, quality, price, changes over time, and whether I would prefer a competing product.

My experience is only one observation. Better Gemini output does not automatically mean higher Alphabet profit. Some businesses, such as enterprise cloud contracts or large-company lending, cannot be meaningfully tested by one retail user. In those cases, I use official customer cases, contract disclosures, and segment results.

Product experience helps create a hypothesis; reported results help test it.

---

## 5. Examine price, P/E, and ROE last

Only after reviewing the business and results do I look at the share price and valuation. JPX defines P/E as the relationship between share price and earnings per share, P/B as the relationship between share price and book value per share, and ROE as profit generated from shareholders' equity.

None is a stand-alone buy signal:

- A low P/E may reflect expected stagnation or declining earnings.
- A high P/E may already price in years of strong growth.
- High ROE may reflect genuine profitability, leverage, or one-time profit.
- Low P/B may reflect poor capital efficiency or doubts about asset value.

I compare a company with its own history, relevant peers, and expected earnings growth. Alphabet and MUFG cannot be judged by the same P/E threshold because their business and capital structures are different.

## My ¥300,000–¥700,000 purchase range and the rule it does not provide

I often choose shares for which an initial purchase can be made with roughly ¥300,000 to ¥700,000. Domestic common stocks trade on Japanese exchanges in units of 100 shares, so a ¥3,000 stock requires about ¥300,000 for one standard lot, while a ¥7,000 stock requires about ¥700,000.

That is an affordability filter, not a position-sizing rule. The same ¥500,000 position has very different consequences in a ¥5 million portfolio and a ¥50 million portfolio.

I do not currently impose a fixed maximum weight for each holding. That is a weakness in my present process. Even without a hard sell threshold, I should check:

- the position's weight after purchase;
- total exposure to the same sector or theme;
- overlap with the largest holdings inside my investment funds; and
- whether a 50% decline would affect living costs or the accumulation plan.

Large market capitalization does not eliminate concentration risk. FINRA notes that concentration in one security, sector, or group of correlated holdings can amplify losses. I may choose not to apply one universal cap, but that is different from ignoring portfolio weight.

---

## I do not wait indefinitely for a lower price

The main reason I delay a purchase is a lack of available cash. I add the company to a watchlist and review it when capital becomes available.

Waiting for a specific decline is often frustrating. The companies that many investors want to own, especially those already trending upward, may never reach the hoped-for price. If I intend to hold for years and the current valuation is reasonable relative to business growth, I may buy without waiting for the perfect entry.

:::comment
In my experience, companies I plan to buy “after they fall” often keep rising. For a business I want to hold long term, I focus on whether today's price is acceptable rather than waiting indefinitely to save a few percent.

The allocation decision is also a comparison between holding cash and owning a productive asset. I sometimes retain more cash when I expect yen appreciation or higher rates, and invest more when rates are stable or falling.
:::

Rate increases do not guarantee yen appreciation or lower stock prices, and the nominal value of cash does not rise automatically. Here, the “value of cash” means its relative appeal after considering interest income, currency, inflation, and expected equity returns. I do not move everything between cash and equities on a macro forecast. Emergency reserves and money needed soon remain separate.

---

## X is where I notice; official filings are where I decide

I generally review Japanese holdings after each set of results. For US holdings, I look more closely after earnings, a large price move, or a meaningful product change. I do not catch every release in real time, and I often first notice results through posts on X.

Social media is useful for discovery, but each post contains selected numbers and interpretations. I keep the following order:

1. Notice the event through X or news.
2. Open the company's IR release, earnings report, or 10-Q.
3. Verify revenue, profit, guidance, and one-time factors.
4. Decide whether the price response genuinely differs from business value.

A positive earnings headline followed by a falling share price is not automatically an opportunity. Results may have missed expectations, guidance may be weak, profit may be temporary, or the starting valuation may have been excessive. Only after excluding those explanations do I consider whether the remaining discrepancy could represent an edge.

:::comment
Large companies attract enough attention that I often learn whether earnings looked positive or negative from X. When results appear good but the stock falls, I investigate whether the market has overlooked something.

I do not trade from the post itself. I check what improved and why the price fell in primary materials before deciding whether there is a genuine valuation gap.
:::

---

## I sell when the business thesis fails, not merely to realize a gain

I generally do not take profit only because the share price has risen substantially. I continue holding while the growth and durability of the business remain intact. Selling a long-term compounder too early can forfeit years of subsequent growth.

I reconsider when:

- the earnings source behind the original purchase weakens across multiple quarters;
- customers lose their reason to choose the service;
- management's capital allocation stops translating profit into shareholder value;
- a clearly more attractive investment requires more capital than new savings can provide; or
- a company or theme grows beyond the loss I can tolerate.

“I will not sell because my unrealized gain is large” is different from “I will hold because the business remains strong.” I need to restate the purchase rationale in the present tense.

## Examples: the sheet stays the same, but the metrics change

| Item | Alphabet | MUFG |
|---|---|---|
| Original reason to buy | YouTube as a major user touchpoint | Opportunities from currency, rates, and new NISA |
| Core thesis today | Cloud, AI, and owned infrastructure | Domestic spreads, overseas operations, and fees |
| Financial metrics | YouTube ads, Cloud profit, capex | Net interest income, spreads, credit costs, CET1 |
| Trigger for adding | Review after a decline of 5% or more | Review business, rates, and forward dividend |
| Invalidation | Advertising and Cloud profit growth breaks | Spreads, credit costs, and capital deteriorate together |

## A one-page investment decision sheet

I have not historically written down my purchase rationale. This is therefore not a form from a past trading journal. It is a suggested template that turns the research process described here into something readers can revisit.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Company and review date | Include the information date |
| Market capitalization | Why this scale belongs in the investable universe |
| Business in one sentence | Who pays the company, and for what |
| Why demand persists for five years | Customers, competitive advantage, and substitution risk |
| Latest results | Revenue, operating profit, cash flow, and thesis-specific metrics |
| Product check | Personal experience or alternative evidence when it cannot be tested |
| Valuation | P/E, ROE, and comparisons with history and peers |
| Why buy now | Why capital should be allocated instead of waiting |
| Amount and post-purchase weight | Record portfolio weight, not only the cash amount |
| Conditions for adding | Combine price with business conditions |
| Invalidation conditions | Specific developments that trigger review or sale |
| Primary sources | URLs for IR materials, filings, 10-Q, or annual reports |

Every field does not need a perfect answer. But if I cannot write “the business in one sentence,” “why buy now,” and “what invalidates the thesis,” the position is likely to become driven by price after purchase.

## Summary

My individual-stock process starts with market cap, then moves through the business, financial results, and product before reaching price and valuation. Because I invest for the long term, I care more about whether demand can persist for five years than whether I can save a few percent on the entry price.

The process still has limitations: I have not used a fixed position cap or recorded each purchase rationale. Recording the post-purchase weight and thesis invalidation conditions is one possible way to address those limitations.

An investment thesis is not only an argument for buying. It is a record of what I will examine when I may be wrong.

This article is not a recommendation to buy or sell any security. Individual stocks can lose value, and each investment decision should reflect the investor's circumstances and risk tolerance.
